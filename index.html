Iron & ferritin — what you actually need to know
Being told "your bloods are fine" when you feel terrible is one of the most common and frustrating experiences with iron deficiency. This page explains why that happens — and why ferritin is the number that actually matters.

This tool provides educational estimates to help you understand iron balance and prepare for a discussion with your GP. It does not diagnose conditions, recommend treatment, or replace medical advice.

This tool is not suitable for urgent symptoms such as severe fatigue, chest pain, shortness of breath, or suspected anaemia — seek medical care promptly.

1. Iron and ferritin are not the same thing
🩸
Haemoglobin
Iron in red blood cells. Carries oxygen around the body. What GPs test for anaemia.
🏪
Ferritin
Iron stored inside cells. Your body's iron reserve — like a depot. This is the number that matters.
🚚
Serum iron
Iron travelling in the blood at that moment. Fluctuates constantly — not reliable on its own.
The key point: You can have normal haemoglobin, normal serum iron, and low ferritin — all at the same time. This is called iron deficiency without anaemia. Your GP may say "your bloods are fine" because haemoglobin looks normal. But ferritin tells a different story.
2. Why you feel terrible even when "bloods are normal"
When iron is in short supply, your body doesn't share it equally. It follows a strict priority order — protecting the most critical functions first, rationing to everything else.
3. Recognising low ferritin symptoms
These symptoms are caused by tissues — particularly brain, muscles, and nerves — not getting enough iron to function properly. They are frequently misattributed to stress, depression, burnout, or "just being tired." If you have several of these alongside low ferritin, they are very likely connected.
Energy & physical
😴Fatigue & low stamina — tired despite sleep, hits a wall
🏃Poor exercise tolerance — out of breath quickly, slow recovery
💓Palpitations or dizziness — heart working harder to compensate
🤕Headaches — particularly tension-type, frequent
❄️Feeling cold — especially hands and feet
Brain & mood
🧠Brain fog — difficulty concentrating, slow thinking, poor memory
😔Low mood or anxiety — iron is needed for neurotransmitter production
😤Irritability — low frustration tolerance, emotional dysregulation
💇Hair loss — telogen effluvium; diffuse shedding, often delayed 2–3 months
🦵Restless legs — uncomfortable urge to move legs, worse at night
Why these symptoms are so often missed: Because haemoglobin looks normal, doctors and patients assume iron is fine. These symptoms get attributed to stress, poor sleep, anxiety, or "just being run down." Many people normalise them and stop mentioning them. The connection to low ferritin is only made when someone specifically tests ferritin — which is not always done as a matter of course.
4. Why people get stuck in a long-term cycle
This pattern is extremely common — and the reason this tool exists.
5. "Not urgent" does not mean "not important"
✓ What is true
Low ferritin is rarely an emergency
It does not usually need same-day treatment
Most cases can be managed in primary care
Symptoms are real and documentable
A plan works — if followed properly
✗ What is also true
"Not urgent" ≠ "fine to ignore"
Doing nothing is not neutral
The body stays in iron-rationing mode
Symptoms become chronic and normalised
Risk of eventual anaemia increases
What chronic low ferritin actually means: The body has been running on minimum iron for an extended period. Brain, muscle, and nerve cells have been receiving the bare minimum to function — not enough for optimal performance. This is not a minor inconvenience. It affects work, mood, physical capacity, and quality of life. It deserves a clear plan, not repeated reassurance.
6. What your ferritin number actually means

Critically depleted — absent iron stores
BSH/WHO diagnostic threshold for iron deficiency. Clinical review recommended before self-managing. Symptoms are expected and significant.
15–30
Depleted stores — symptoms likely
Below BSH depleted stores threshold. Iron deficiency is present even if haemoglobin is normal. Supplementation and monitoring indicated.
30–50
Below optimal — symptoms may persist
Some iron reserve present but below the level at which most people feel well. Symptoms of fatigue, brain fog and hair loss commonly persist in this range.
50–100
Recommended minimum — most symptoms resolve
BSH recommended minimum for menstruating women. At this level tissues receive adequate iron. Most people notice meaningful improvement in energy, cognition and mood.
100+
Well-repleted — optimal buffer
Comfortable iron reserve. Typically achieved after IV infusion. Provides headroom against ongoing monthly losses. Maintenance dosing keeps it stable.
Many people do not feel consistently well until ferritin reaches 70–100 µg/L — even though labs may report anything above 13 as "normal."

► ℹ How are these numbers calculated?
÷ 8 — ferritin ↔ iron stores

1 µg/L of serum ferritin = approximately 8 mg of stored iron. This is a validated clinical conversion used in haematology to estimate total body iron stores from a blood test result.

0.5 mg/ml — iron in menstrual blood

Menstrual blood contains approximately 0.5 mg of iron per ml of blood lost. Used to convert your flow volume inputs into monthly iron loss in mg.

30 mg/month — basal iron loss

The average person loses approximately 1 mg of iron per day through skin cell shedding, gut lining renewal, and sweat. This is a fixed baseline used in clinical iron balance models.

4% — dietary absorption baseline

The average absorption rate for non-haem iron from a typical Western diet. Haem iron (meat) absorbs at 15–35%; non-haem at 1–10% depending on diet and gut health. 4% is the standard clinical estimate for a mixed diet.

?
Both are prescribed iron supplements available on the NHS. The difference is the tablet weight and how much actual iron each contains.
Ferrous sulfate 200mg — 65mg actual iron per tablet. Most commonly prescribed. Can cause nausea, constipation or dark stools in some people, especially on an empty stomach.

Ferrous fumarate 210mg — 69mg actual iron per tablet. Marginally more iron per tablet. Generally considered slightly better tolerated than sulfate, though evidence is mixed. A useful alternative if sulfate causes GI side effects.

Ferrous bisglycinate 20mg — 20mg actual iron per capsule. OTC only (not NHS prescribed). Chelated to glycine so does not release free iron in the gut — dramatically fewer side effects. Absorption is estimated to be better than standard iron salts per mg (typically 1.5–2× higher, though this varies by dose and individual — the evidence base is less robust than for sulfate/fumarate). Best for sensitive stomachs, maintenance, or those who cannot tolerate prescribed iron. Sold as Gentle Iron, OptiFer, and similar brands.
Ferrous (Fe²⁺) forms absorb significantly better than ferric (Fe³⁺) supplements. Enteric-coated or slow-release tablets are NOT recommended — they bypass the stomach where most absorption occurs. Liquid iron sachets (Spatone etc.) contain only 5mg actual iron and are only suitable if maintenance needs are very low — check the Tolerability tab.
⚠️ Above 3 tablets (195mg actual iron) per day, absorption is unlikely to increase further. Hepcidin rises after each dose and blocks the intestinal iron transporter for approximately 24 hours — extra tablets pass through unabsorbed and increase the risk of side effects (nausea, constipation). Taking a lower dose every other day is likely to deliver equal or better results. Source: Moretti et al, Lancet Haematology 2015.

Other factors affecting absorption (reference notes)

Formulation: Ferrous forms (sulfate, fumarate, bisglycinate) absorb better than ferric. Bisglycinate may absorb 2-4x better than sulfate with fewer GI side effects.

Alternate-day dosing: Daily iron raises hepcidin, suppressing next-day absorption. Every-other-day dosing can yield similar or better total absorption with fewer side effects. Source: Moretti et al, Lancet Haematology 2015.

Hepcidin and inflammation: Active infection or inflammation raises hepcidin, blocking absorption regardless of dose. Check CRP alongside ferritin — elevated CRP can cause falsely reassuring ferritin readings.

H. pylori: Impairs gastric acid and competes for iron. Refractory deficiency not responding to supplements warrants H. pylori testing. Source: NICE CG184.

Phytates: Found in wholegrains, legumes, nuts, seeds. Bind non-haem iron strongly. Especially relevant for vegetarian/vegan diets.

Getting consistent blood test results

Iron test results can vary significantly depending on when and how you test. Following these guidelines each time gives the most comparable, reliable readings:

✓Test at the same point in your cycle — ferritin dips during and just after bleeding, then recovers. Mid-cycle (around day 10–18 depending on cycle length) gives the most stable reading. Testing at different cycle points makes results hard to compare.
✓Avoid testing during illness or infection — ferritin is an acute phase reactant: it rises when your body is fighting inflammation, which can mask true iron stores and make levels appear higher than they are. Wait at least 2–3 weeks after illness.
✓Morning tests preferred — slight diurnal variation means morning samples are marginally more consistent, especially if comparing to previous results taken at the same time.
ℹFasting is not required for ferritin — unlike serum iron or transferrin saturation, ferritin is not significantly affected by meals. No need to fast.
ℹSupplement timing does not affect ferritin — ferritin reflects long-term iron stores, not recent dose timing. Taking your supplement the day before or day of the test makes no meaningful difference.
★Request CRP alongside ferritin if possible — C-reactive protein (CRP) is a marker of inflammation. If CRP is elevated at the same time as ferritin, the ferritin result may be falsely high and not reflect true iron stores. Knowing both together makes the ferritin result much easier to interpret.

Section 3 — Worth knowing
🏃 Exercise — be aware, not alarmed
Moderate exercise is beneficial overall and does not impair iron absorption. However heavy endurance training (marathon running, cycling, triathlon) increases iron demand through three mechanisms: foot-strike haemolysis (red blood cells destroyed on impact), sweat iron losses, and elevated hepcidin after intense sessions. If you train heavily and have refractory deficiency, exercise-related iron loss may be a contributing factor worth mentioning to your GP.
😴 Sleep & stress
Poor sleep and chronic stress both elevate inflammatory markers (particularly IL-6), which in turn raise hepcidin and impair iron absorption. The effect is modest compared to heavy periods or gut inflammation, but over time it compounds. Good sleep and stress management support iron recovery — not as a replacement for supplementation, but as a genuine contributor. This is modelled in the inflammation toggle above.
🦠 H. pylori — worth testing if nothing else works
H. pylori infection impairs gastric acid production and directly competes for iron at the gut wall. It is a frequently overlooked cause of refractory iron deficiency — meaning deficiency that does not respond to supplementation as expected. If ferritin is not recovering despite good adherence and diet, a simple breath test or stool antigen test for H. pylori is worth requesting. Successful treatment can substantially improve iron absorption long-term.
🍳 Cast iron cookware
Cooking acidic foods (tomatoes, citrus sauces, stews) in cast iron pans leaches small amounts of iron into the food — typically 1–3mg per serving depending on cooking time and acidity. This is non-haem iron with moderate bioavailability. It is a genuine if modest contribution — roughly equivalent to adding a small portion of iron-rich vegetables to a meal. Worth doing, but in the context of a 30–40mg/month deficit from heavy periods, the impact is limited.
🥛 Lactoferrin — emerging evidence
Bovine lactoferrin is a protein found in colostrum and dairy that appears to improve iron absorption by a different pathway to standard supplementation — it is less affected by hepcidin. Small studies suggest it may improve ferritin in iron-deficient women, with fewer GI side effects than ferrous sulfate. Evidence is not yet strong enough for mainstream recommendation, but it is increasingly used and discussed. Worth mentioning to your GP if standard oral iron is poorly tolerated.
🌿 Commonly claimed but evidence is weak
Blackstrap molasses, spirulina, nettle tea, and beetroot are often promoted for iron. All contain some iron, but bioavailability is generally poor — the food matrix (phytates, oxalates) inhibits absorption significantly. These are not harmful, but the effect on ferritin in a clinically deficient person is negligible compared to supplementation or dietary haem iron. Spinach is a good example: it contains iron but also oxalates that bind it — you absorb very little. Lightly cooked spinach with citrus dressing absorbs better than raw, but it is still non-haem iron with limited effect on stores.
Side effects & tolerability
GI side effects are the most common reason people stop taking iron — and stopping is the worst outcome. This page explains why they happen, what you can actually do about them, and how much each compromise costs in terms of absorption.
Why does iron upset the stomach?
🦠
Unabsorbed iron
Iron that isn't absorbed stays in the gut and reacts with gut bacteria — producing inflammation, gas and cramping
🏔️
Dose too high
The higher the dose, the more unabsorbed iron reaches the lower gut. Most side effects are a dose problem, not an iron problem
⏰
Timing matters
Iron taken at night sits in the gut for 8 hours when motility is slowest — concentrating irritation. Morning is significantly better
The good news: Side effects are not inevitable. They are strongly dose-dependent and formulation-dependent — and can usually be managed without stopping treatment. The strategies below are evidence-based and practical.

Practical strategies — ranked by effectiveness
BIGGEST IMPACT Switch to alternate-day dosing
Taking iron every other day instead of daily dramatically reduces gut exposure — you're absorbing a similar amount but exposing the gut to iron half as often. Most people find this resolves or greatly reduces side effects. The absorption benefit (hepcidin reset) means you may actually absorb more iron per dose. See the compromise chart above — this often has a positive or neutral effect on trajectory.
BIGGEST IMPACT Switch to ferrous bisglycinate (Gentle Iron)
Bisglycinate is chelated to glycine, which means it doesn't release free iron in the gut — the mechanism that causes most irritation. Multiple studies show significantly fewer GI side effects vs sulfate, with comparable or better absorption per mg. Available OTC (Holland & Barrett, Amazon etc.) — not prescribed on NHS but not expensive. Typical dose: 20–25mg actual iron per capsule. Not suitable for high-dose recovery without many capsules, but excellent for maintenance or sensitive stomachs.
MODERATE IMPACT Take in the morning, not evening
Iron taken at night sits in the gut for 6–8 hours when gut motility is at its lowest. This concentrates the irritation. Taking iron in the morning — ideally before food or at breakfast — allows normal gut activity to move it through more quickly. Morning also has a slight absorption advantage as hepcidin levels are lowest in the morning. No absorption cost — this is simply better practice.
MODERATE IMPACT Take with a small amount of food (if needed)
Taking iron with food significantly reduces nausea for most people. The absorption cost is real — food reduces absorption by approximately 30–40%. However as the compromise chart shows, even with this reduction ferritin will still recover — just more slowly. If the choice is between iron-with-food-consistently vs iron-empty-stomach-occasionally, the former usually results in better outcomes because adherence is maintained. A small amount of food (e.g. a piece of toast) is better than a full meal — the inhibition scales with meal size. Vitamin C alongside partially counteracts the food effect.
MODERATE IMPACT Start low and build up
Starting at 1 tablet every other day for the first 2–3 weeks gives the gut microbiome time to adapt to the change in iron exposure. The gut bacteria population shifts in response to increased iron — and this transition is often the most symptomatic period. Once adapted, many people tolerate full dosing. Building up gradually extends the recovery timeline by a few weeks but dramatically improves tolerability and adherence.
MODERATE IMPACT Take with a full glass of water
Water dilutes the iron concentration in the stomach and speeds transit through the upper GI tract. A full glass (250ml+) significantly reduces local concentration and irritation. This is low-cost, no absorption penalty, and easy to implement. Also helps with constipation — a common complaint.
SMALL BUT HELPFUL Add vitamin C to the tablet
Ascorbic acid (vitamin C) not only improves absorption — it also helps keep iron in the soluble ferrous (Fe²⁺) state that is less reactive in the gut. Reactive ferric (Fe³⁺) iron is responsible for much of the gut irritation. Taking 200mg vitamin C alongside the iron tablet reduces irritation slightly while improving absorption. A win-win. Orange juice works too.
AVOID Enteric-coated tablets or slow-release iron
These are often marketed as "gentler on the stomach" and they are — because the coating bypasses the stomach (where most iron absorption occurs) and releases the iron in the intestine where absorption is much poorer. You get fewer side effects because you're absorbing less iron. This defeats the purpose. If tolerability is the issue, alternate-day dosing or bisglycinate are better solutions.
The minimum effective approach — if everything else has failed
If full-dose daily iron is genuinely not tolerable, this is the most effective reduced-impact approach that still achieves meaningful results — based on your current balance model settings.
Important: If oral iron in any form is not tolerated, this should be documented and discussed with your GP. IV iron infusion is clinically indicated for oral iron intolerance and is highly effective — it delivers iron directly into the bloodstream, completely bypassing the gut.
